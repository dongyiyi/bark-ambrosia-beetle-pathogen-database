# Methods and scope

## Taxonomic scope

The database focuses on bark and ambrosia beetles in the subfamily **Scolytinae**. It does not currently attempt to represent all ambrosia beetles globally, because ambrosia systems also occur outside Scolytinae.

## Literature basis

Records are extracted from primary literature. Reviews, books, book chapters, and synthesis papers may be used to locate or cross-check source literature, but database records are derived from primary sources wherever possible.

The literature search covers publications from 1914 through 2026 and was closed on 24 July 2026 for Version 1.0.

## Inclusion logic

The database retains literature-reported organisms associated with Scolytinae beetles and preserves the ecological status assigned or supported by the source. For fungi and bacteria, records reported only as associates or symbionts are retained but are coded as **associate** rather than as pathogen records unless the source provides evidence or explicitly identifies a pathogenic relationship. Nematodes, protists, microsporidia, and viruses are likewise retained with the reported ecological relationship preserved.

This structure allows reported association to remain distinct from infection, pathogenicity, virulence, and other forms of experimental validation.

## Record unit

Each row represents a unique literature-derived organism–beetle association within a country or region in a publication. Rows are not independent biological replicates and should not be interpreted as sample sizes or infection events.

## Standardization

Taxonomic names are reconciled to accepted nomenclature where feasible while retaining source fidelity. Reconciliation does not constitute re-identification of specimens.

## Evidence architecture

The database separates the ecological relationship reported by the original authors from the presence or absence of experimental validation. Evidence categories include infection, pathogenicity, virulence, and related tests when explicitly reported.

## Important limitations

Research effort is uneven across taxa, geography, time, and methodological traditions. Record counts therefore reflect both biological reporting and research intensity. Absence of a database record does not imply biological absence.
